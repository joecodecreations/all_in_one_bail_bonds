How bail works in North Carolina

A plain-language walk through what happens after an arrest in NC: who sets the bond, the kinds of release, what a bail bondsman does and what the cosigner agrees to.

The short version

After an arrest, a court official decides the conditions for release until the case is over. If the condition is a secured bond, money or property has to back it. A licensed bail bondsman can post that bond for a fee called the premium, which North Carolina caps at 15% of the bond amount. The person is released and must go to every court date.

Step 1: The arrest and the magistrate

After booking, the person is taken before a magistrate, who sets the conditions of pretrial release in most cases. In a few situations the law says a judge has to set them instead, and the person can be held for up to 48 hours until a judge is available. That applies to domestic violence charges and to certain serious felonies and new charges picked up while already on pretrial release.

Step 2: The kinds of release

North Carolina's pretrial release statute (G.S. 15A-534) lists the options a court can use:

  • Unsecured bond. No money up front. The person owes the amount only if they miss court.
  • Custody release. The person is released into the supervision of a named person or organization.
  • Secured bond. The bond must be backed by a cash deposit of the full amount, a mortgage on property, or a licensed bail bondsman acting as surety.
  • House arrest with electronic monitoring, which also requires a secured bond.

Changed December 1, 2025: a written promise to appear is no longer a release option in North Carolina. The same law requires a secured bond or monitored house arrest for defendants with three or more convictions for Class 1 misdemeanors or higher in the last 10 years, and it tightens release for charges the statute defines as violent offenses.

Step 3: Posting a secured bond

You have three ways to satisfy a secured bond: pay the full amount in cash to the court, pledge property, or hire a bail bondsman. Most families use a bondsman because they do not have the full amount on hand. With a bondsman you pay the premium, and the bondsman guarantees the full bond to the court.

Step 4: The paperwork

The cosigner signs an agreement taking responsibility for the defendant showing up in court. For larger bonds the bondsman may ask for collateral. NC law requires a written receipt describing any collateral, and requires it to be returned within 15 days after the bondsman's liability on the bond finally ends.

Step 5: Release, and what comes after

The bondsman posts the bond with the jail and the jail processes the release. From then on the most important thing is simple: go to every court date. When the case ends, the bond is discharged. The premium is not refunded, because it paid for the service.

If a court date is missed, call your bondsman immediately. See what happens after a missed court date.

Common questions

What is a secured bond?

A secured bond means money or property has to back the bond before the person is released. It can be secured with a cash deposit of the full amount, with a mortgage on property, or by a licensed bail bondsman acting as surety. An unsecured bond needs no money up front; the person owes the amount only if they miss court.

Who decides the bond amount?

A magistrate sets release conditions soon after most arrests. For some cases, including certain serious felonies and domestic violence charges, North Carolina law says a judge must set the conditions, and the person can be held up to 48 hours until a judge does.

Did the law on pretrial release change recently?

Yes. Since December 1, 2025, a written promise to appear is no longer a release option in North Carolina. Courts must also order a secured bond or house arrest with electronic monitoring for defendants with three or more convictions for Class 1 misdemeanors or higher in the past 10 years, and release rules are stricter for charges the law defines as violent offenses.

What does the person who signs the bond agree to?

The cosigner (also called the indemnitor) promises that the defendant will go to every court date. If the defendant misses court and the bond is forfeited, the cosigner can be responsible for the full bond amount.

Do I get the premium back when the case is over?

No. The premium is the fee for the bondsman's service and for taking on the risk of the full bond, so it is not returned when the case ends. Collateral is different: under NC law it must be returned within 15 days after the bondsman's liability on the bond finally ends.

This guide is general information about North Carolina's bail process, not legal advice. For advice about a specific case, talk to a licensed attorney. Statutes referenced: N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 15A-533, 15A-534, 15A-534.1, 58-71-95 and 58-71-100.
